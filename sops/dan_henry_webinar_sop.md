# Dan Henry Webinar SOP

Everything Dan Henry teaches about selling with webinars, pulled from his own words.

**Who he is:** $40M+ in sales from webinars and presentations over 13 years. WSJ bestselling author. Runs GetClients.com, 3,000+ clients. Does done-for-you webinar scripting for eight-figure clients. Sells "The Million Dollar Webinar Workshop".

**Sources:** 40 of his YouTube videos (auto-transcribed and mined), 10 of his Instagram reels (downloaded and transcribed with Whisper), 101 reel captions, and his own MDW sales page. Scraped 2026-08-05.

**How to read this:** quotes in "quotation marks" are his exact words. Everything else is a faithful paraphrase of what he actually said. Nothing here is invented.

---

## 1. The one idea underneath everything

> "When you do a webinar, you don't want to do a seminar. You want to do a selling presentation, because you want people at the end of it to actually buy. These are two fundamentally different ways to communicate."

That is the whole thesis. A seminar informs. A selling presentation sells. Most people accidentally build a seminar and then wonder why nobody buys.

His test of which one you built:

> "If it's a good webinar, people buy. If it's a bad webinar, they go 'wow, that's amazing stuff' and they don't buy. And if it's a terrible webinar, then they don't even like the webinar."

**"Wow, amazing stuff" with no sales is a failure, not a compliment.**

---

## 2. The gap mechanism (the actual engine)

His cigarette-and-forest analogy, verbatim in substance:

Drop a cigarette in a forest 100 times and it will not start a fire 100 times, because the conditions are usually wrong. For the fire to spread you need wind, dryness, the right conditions. Same with a webinar:

> "If you want people to say 'oh my gosh, that's a no brainer offer, I have to buy', you can't just guess. You have to create the conditions for that to happen. And the conditions are: you present a way of thinking, a process, a way to get what they want, that creates natural gaps in their mind, and you fill those gaps with an offer."

So the job of your content is **not** to complete their understanding. It is to open specific, deliberate gaps that your offer is the natural answer to.

> "Your content in a webinar should set up gaps that the offer fills. So the natural question everybody has is, hey, can I get help with this?"

**Practical rule he states directly:**

> "The webinar mistake that kills sales: teaching the 'how.' Teach what, why, and when. Save the how for the offer."

## 3. Green flags and red flags (his own checklist)

Straight from his green flag / red flag reel.

**Green flags**
- People stay to the very end.
- A single clear promise up front. "If I know exactly why I'm on a webinar, I'm going to stay. But if it's muddied, I'm going to leave."
- The offer feels like the obvious next step.
- One irresistible offer, "not three confusing ones." "You always want to sell one clear offer on your webinar. You don't want to give options."
- One story that makes them feel understood. "One of the best things you can do on a webinar is show people that you understand their problem better than they do. That's when they're going to buy."
- **The biggest green flag of all:** "About 10 minutes before the pitch, you should have people getting annoyed because they want to hear the offer."

**Red flags**
- Ten minutes on your credentials. "Nobody cares about your bio. They care about their problem."
- An origin story about you. "Your origin story should be about how you found the solution to their problem. Not about you."
- "You teach so much they leave satisfied and never buy."
- Winging it. "You can't wing a webinar. It's just not going to happen, bro."
- No deadline, no reason to act now. "Human psychology states that people buy when there's a deadline. Period. Do not try to break a multi thousand year old psychology rule."
- "Crickets the moment you say the price. If you did not build the value, people aren't going to feel like it's a steal. And when people feel like it's a steal, that's when they buy."

## 4. What actually matters, and what does not

From his "important or not" reel. This is his priority ranking and it is blunt.

**Not important**
- **Perfect slides.** "Pretty slides don't close." But: "the slides should amplify the most important points of the script. That's the job of slides, to keep people focused on what you say."
- **High production and lighting.** "Especially in the beginning, your phone or a webcam is totally fine."
- **A word-for-word teleprompter.** Read off your screen with the webcam at the top, or go off the cuff if you know the material.
- **Fake scarcity.** "Please never do it. People are not stupid... If you try to trick them with fake urgency, it's not going to work."

**Important**
- **Your personal story,** but only "as long as it's about how you discovered the method that you're going to teach them to solve their problem."
- **A killer offer stack.** "They have to know what they're getting. If they're unsure, they're never going to apply, book a call or buy."
- **A single clear promise.** "Confused people do not buy."
- **A real deadline.** "No reason to act now means they will never act."
- **Showing proof.** If you have none: "give your product away for free to your friends or existing clients just to get some reviews to show on your webinar. And then as you sell, you just keep updating that webinar with better and better proof. That's what I did. And one of my first banger webinars did $9.2 million."
- **Live Q&A,** to address objections (see section 9 for his trick).

His summary of what makes a webinar print:

> "A real story, one clear promise, and an offer they'd be crazy to pass on."

## 5. Structure and running order

This is his actual named structure, from "Step-by-Step How I Write $1M Webinars Over and Over". He says he studied **214 webinars that each made over $1M** before codifying it, and has done **$35M** with it himself.

**Length:** ~40 to 45 minutes of presentation. He describes his own as a "45-minute Zoom with a single script."

**"The overall structure of a webinar" (his words):**

1. **Welcome.** Skip the chit-chat. He explicitly says do **not** open with "what city are you from?" Start with a strong hook.
2. **Expectations.** "Here is what you will learn in this webinar... so you don't leave."
3. **Discovery.** The origin story: "I was in this place that you were in... I figured out how to solve this problem."
4. **Value sections.** Usually **three**. "Three is a great number. Tony Robbins does three in almost everything he does." Each section covers **what, why and when**, and **leaves the how for the offer**.
5. **Transition.** The two-option script (section 5b).
6. **Offer, then bonuses.** Present the offer right after the transition, then give the bonuses to strengthen it.
7. **Q&A.** Surface and answer objections, and keep selling. "I sat there for the next two hours, answered questions, overcame objections, and got it to $200k."

### 5a. The Anchor (first 5 to 30 seconds)

His named concept for the opening. An **anchor** is proof, dropped immediately, that makes leaving feel stupid.

> "You see these payments right here? These are payments I got paid for work that I did not do."

The goal, in his words, is to "attach an anchor to my belt" so the viewer thinks "I can't go, this is a webinar I should stay for."

**Anchor types he lists:** a Stripe screenshot with a live login, before-and-after photos, a house purchase with the wire transfer, a divorce notice. Real, specific, verifiable.

### 5b. The two-option transition (his exact script)

This is how he moves from teaching to pitching without a gear change. Learn it word for word.

> "You have **two options** at this point. You can take everything I taught you during this training and go out and do it yourself... or **number two**, you could have somebody who has already done it walk you through it, hold your hand, or do it for you. **Which would you choose? One or two? Let me know in the chat.**"

The room types "two". Then:

> "Since you all said two, **do you mind if I share this offer with you to do just that?**"

The audience has now asked him to pitch. He notes that if you are still nervous after the room has said "sell me", "you have problems."

### 5c. The Path to Success framework

From "I made $35M with webinars". This is the shape of the teaching content itself.

1. **Explain the painful problem.** "The best ways to produce traffic are to talk about the painful problem."
2. **Show the other paths** and why they fail: old, hard, long, scary, risky, unbelievable, ineffective.
3. **Present your path** as explicitly none of those things.
4. **Transition to the offer,** which is always one of three things: "take the path **for** them, **with** them, or **make it easier or faster**."

His positioning script, verbatim:

> "Hey, let me show you how a foundation can completely eliminate your taxes. Here's what it is. Here's why it works, and here's when to use it."

Note the ending: what, why, when. Never how.

**Pacing:**

> "You can't just jibber jabber on and on and on. You gotta be quick, you gotta keep people's attention and you gotta move quick."

**A real example of his own timing,** from his behind-the-scenes build video: about 14 minutes of advice, then the core content, then the pitch at the end, with a literal **countdown slide** signalling the switch ("Dan's advice ends and study begins"). He builds it by googling "countdown", screen recording it and overlaying it.

**A real example of his own timing,** from his behind-the-scenes build video:

> "Up until about, I think it's about like 14 minutes, I give advice on what I've learned, and then right around 14 minutes we start the study, and then at the end I make a pitch."

He signals that transition with a literal **countdown slide**: "it says 'Dan's advice ends and study begins' and it counts down." He builds it by googling "countdown", screen recording it, and overlaying it.

**Pacing:**

> "You can't just jibber jabber on and on and on. You gotta be quick, you gotta keep people's attention and you gotta move quick."

## 6. Hooks and openings

**His definition of a hook:**

> "Something someone sees or hears that creates multiple burning questions in their mind that they cannot stand to go unanswered."

**Hook tactics he uses:**
- **Contrarian statement**, then explain yourself. Example of his own: "I've made $40 million from my personal brand. My number one piece of advice is to stop growing an audience."
- **Bold stat or study.** "According to this MIT study, if you get less than 6 hours of sleep per night as a man, it's 300% harder for you to lose weight."
- **Relatable opening question.** "Ever wish you had more hours in the day?"
- **Curiosity, controversy, conflict, or contrast.** Make them feel "that's me" or "we were just talking about this yesterday."
- **A qualifying offer-style hook.** "If I could get you just one new client every month for $97 a month, would that be worth it?"

**Re-hooks.** Do not hook once. "Rehooks are things that are put throughout the video to retain attention and keep you in there."

**Anticipate the next question.** After a bold claim, ask the audience's mental question out loud and then answer it. "What does that mean?"

**Pattern interrupts.** The classic: "Hey, you might want to write this down."

**Doctrine framing.** Label your core belief as a doctrine: "Make this your Doctrine." (He credits Dan Kennedy's terminology.)

## 7. Story: Pain, Process, Proof, Pivot

The four-part pitch framework. He learned it in the same mastermind as Alex Hormozi, who used it to do $105M in a day. Dan made $40M with it.

> "It's pain, process, proof, pivot. You start with your worst moment. You show the specific steps you took, the proof that it worked, and then you pivot to how you can do it too."

**Why it works:** the gap again.

> "If you've ever heard him say 'oh, I was $40,000 in debt living on the couch and now my portfolio company's made $100 million'. This is by design because it creates a natural gap that makes the average person look at it and go, well, how did he get there? This is how you get people to beg you to sell them something without you ever having to ask."

**The story rule:** it must be about how you found the solution to *their* problem, not about you.

## 8. The three ingredients of a pitch

From his $5K pitch teardown. He stresses ingredients over templates.

1. **Pain.** "The pain must not be what they say, but what they feel."
2. **Big Idea.** "A mythical creature in the marketing and sales world" that "shifts how people think." His example: **"muscle confusion"** from P90X.
3. **Promise.** "A clear-cut, very specific promise in one sentence." Example: "I will help you wake up at 50 looking better than you did at 40."

Then the simple spine: **explain the problem, explain the solution, make the offer.**

> "People buy from people who understand their problem better than they do."

### The E.P.I.C. framework

His named acronym for the four qualities every winning sales presentation must have. From "This pitch made me $32,000,000".

- **Entertaining.** "People really don't want to be taught anything, they want to be entertained."
- **Personalized.** "It has to sound like it was made exactly for them."
- **Irrefutable.** Align with what the audience already believes *before* you make your claim. His example: open by asking "what is the most valuable computer company?" and letting them answer Apple, so your following point lands on ground they already agreed to.
- **Clear.** "Stupid simple." Small words, one easy-to-remember message.

### The three pitch lengths

- **Micro pitch.** One sentence, elevator style. His formula, verbatim: **"I help [blank] get [blank] without [blank]."** His own: "I help online entrepreneurs create sales presentations that get them clients for life without sales calls or a sales team."
- **Short pitch.** A VSL of 5 to 10 minutes. Use it either for a cheap product they can buy immediately, or for an expensive product that will be followed by a sales call.
- **Long pitch.** The webinar, 1 to 2 hours, live or recorded, reusable forever.

### Offer construction: always show a path

1. Show where the prospect is now.
2. Show the desired end state.
3. Offer to get them there **easier or faster**: done for you, done with you, or software.

## 9. Objections: prevent, do not handle

His stated philosophy:

> "Every objection you hear means you missed something earlier, so I prevent them instead of handling them."

**The Q&A trick.** This is one of his most actionable tactics:

> "I recommend the first four questions that you answer on a live Q&A at the end of your webinar are pre-planned questions that answer universal objections. Like, why is it so expensive? And what if I can't find the time? What if I need to talk to my spouse."

**Testimonials as objection handling.** After the CTA:

> "I play video testimonials from my clients at the end of every webinar for sometimes 15, 20, 30 minutes."

Pick high-contrast stories. His examples: a client who went "from 150 grand a month to 750 grand a month", another "from $500 to a $12.5 million annual contract".

**The five magic words** for any objection on a call:

> "What makes you say that."

Then solve the actual objection they reveal. His example: prospect says "I don't have time for that", you ask what makes them say that, they explain, you respond "if I add three weeks to your coaching contract, would that work for you?"

**Price objection scripts:**
- "Are you saying that it's worth [old price] to [achieve goal], but it's not worth [new price]?"
- "Is your marriage and your kids' rooms worth $15,000?"
- "How can we make it happen for you?"
- "Isn't that why you're on the call in the first place?"
- On price rises: "do not acknowledge this", "focus on the result". "We've raised our prices because our students get results."
- "I do not help losers, I help winners win bigger. Losers focus on [price], winners focus on [results]."
- On discount hunters: "You don't want to work with people with coupon mentality."

## 10. Closing

**The question close.** His single best closing idea:

> "I used the question close. If you ate nothing for 16 hours, what do you think would happen? And I let the audience tell me... I let them give me the conclusion, so they believe it more than I do. They argue with themselves on why they didn't invest sooner."

The broke version pushes the conclusion at them loudly. The rich version asks the question and lets them say it.

**Delivery.** Do not get louder, get quieter:

> "I get quieter right before the most important line... I use silence as a tool. Two seconds before the most important line. Abraham Lincoln, Winston Churchill, and the greatest speakers alive and dead, all knew this."

> "I leave the stage calm, and the audience leaves with my product."

**Give them time to decide:**

> "You can't just say 'alright book a call' or 'alright buy' and then the webinar ends. You got to give them some time to be like, should I do this."

**One CTA.** "There is a golden rule of never have two offers, never have two CTAs, and it's a rule that you really shouldn't break." (He notes when he deliberately tests breaking it.)

**Real deadlines only.** See the fake scarcity ban above.

## 11. Price

**Build value before the number.** If there are crickets when you say the price, you did not build value. "When people feel like it's a steal, that's when they buy."

**Visual anchoring.** On screenshots of client results, he puts arrows on the price jumps to force the eye: "from 997 to 5000... he doubled his price to ten thousand... from charging 500 to a minimum of a hundred thousand."

**Charge on results, not on contents.** "You charge based on the result you're getting." His example: the same curriculum sells at $997 as a course and $5,000+ as a coaching program.

**Raise price by adding support, not modules.** He describes going $2,000 to $5,000 to $10,000 largely by adding coaching calls and changing what is said on the phone.

## 12. The offer

**Four levels of offer,** his scale of how much pain the problem causes:
1. **Must-be-nice** offers. Not very painful.
2. **Annoyance** offers. Annoying but not critical.
3. **Insomnia** offers. Keeps them awake at night.
4. **Bleeding neck** offers. Urgent and critical.

The same offer moves up and down this scale depending on **audience**. Bookkeeping is must-be-nice for a family and insomnia for an ecommerce owner. Sell into insomnia and bleeding neck.

**Quantifiable End Result (QER).** The promise must be measurable.
- Bad: "be a better speaker".
- Good: "land your first paid gig", "double your price", "get paid $10,000 to $15,000 per speech".
- "If the promise is not quantifiable, marketing will not be scalable."

**The offer must be understood.** "They have to know what they're getting. If they're unsure, they're never going to apply, book a call or buy."

**Sell a partnership, not a saviour.** "Stop selling yourself as the magic solution, sell a partnership, and your clients will never blame you when they don't do the work."

**Sell what they think they need.** "You never sell what you think people need. You sell them what in their mind they think they need, but once they get it, they realize, 'oh, I actually need this.'"

## 13. Funnel economics: charge for the webinar

One of his strongest current positions, with his real numbers.

> "You see this number $419,000? That's what we made in the last 30 days with one webinar... about $247,000 in profit."

The logic: a free webinar means you are in the hole on ads before anyone shows up. A **paid** webinar with a bump and an upsell returns "anywhere from 80% to 120% of your money back before they even attend the webinar. And then anything that you make on that webinar is pure profit." He ran $130,000 in ads against that $419,000.

**What charging does to your room:**
- "You're going to get almost 100% show up."
- "You're going to get buyers on the webinar, not just freebie seekers."
- Higher application rate, higher close rate on the calls booked from it.

> "The #1 hack to get near-100% webinar show-up rates: charge for it."

He also frames Tony Robbins this way: "he does presentations... they are paid, and he ascends you to the next presentation."

## 14. His hard rules

- Do not build a seminar. Build a selling presentation.
- Teach the what, why and when. Never give away the how.
- One promise. One offer. One CTA.
- Never fake scarcity. Always a real deadline.
- Never wing it. Run a real script.
- Do not open with your credentials, and do not open with "what city are you from?" Open with an anchor.
- Do not tell your story about you. Tell it about how you found their solution.
- Build value before you say a number.
- Prevent objections in the content instead of handling them at the end.
- Give them time to decide before you end.
- Make them ask for the pitch with the two-option transition.

## 15. Diagnostic: is our webinar built to sell?

Run this against any deck before it goes live.

- [ ] Can a stranger state the single promise after the first two minutes?
- [ ] Does the story explain how we found *their* solution, or is it a biography?
- [ ] Does every teaching section open a gap, or does it close one for free?
- [ ] Have we taught any complete "how" that removes the need to buy?
- [ ] Ten minutes before the pitch, would the audience be impatient for the offer?
- [ ] Is there exactly one offer and one CTA?
- [ ] Is the deadline real?
- [ ] Is value built before the price appears?
- [ ] Are the first four Q&A questions pre-planned to kill universal objections?
- [ ] Do we ask questions and let them draw the conclusion, or do we assert it at them?
- [ ] Is there real proof on screen, even if borrowed from free clients?
- [ ] Is there an anchor in the first 30 seconds, or do we warm up first?
- [ ] Are there three clear value sections, each what/why/when only?
- [ ] Does the audience ask us to pitch, or do we just start pitching?

## Appendix: other named concepts he uses

- **Agreement engineering.** His term for sequencing micro-agreements so that "not buying would mean disagreeing with everything they've already nodded yes to." Sold inside the Million Dollar Webinar Workshop.
- **The Big Domino.** The single belief the entire presentation must install.
- **The Goldfish Rule.** A nine-second test every ad, email and headline must pass.
- **Nut and bolt offers.** Small, specific, tangible offers to get a foot in the door. Example: a $37 course on installing a Facebook pixel.
- **Narrow then broad.** Start with narrow specific content, widen later.
- **Simplification over teaching.** "Simplify things for your audience, don't try to teach them new stuff."
- **Intent over curiosity.** "People that are curious are window shoppers, people that buy based on intent are clients."
- **Go deep, not wide.** "You do not need a wider net, you need a deeper well."
- **Beta launch / the 70% rule.** Sell the course before creating it, teach it live, edit the recordings into the product.
- **The testimonial hack.** Ask at the end of a call when "their energy level is gonna be at an all-time high": "what did you think about today's lesson, and what would you say to somebody considering enrolling in this program?"
- **You don't need a different offer, you need a different pitch.** "I've made 40 million, but I have a unique way to explain it. You don't need a unique offer, you need a unique explanation."
