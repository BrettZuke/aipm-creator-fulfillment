# Email Format Reference — the Shelby Sapp pattern library

> A teardown of one operator's email FORMAT, taken from a run of her published sends. Shelby Sapp
> runs She Sells Academy, US sales coaching for women.
>
> **Purpose:** this is a study of structure, not of voice. Her register is American and
> twenty-something; almost no client shares it, and the slang does not transfer. The structure
> does, and that is what is catalogued below: pacing, punctuation, where emphasis lands, how a
> pivot is set up. Read it when a client's emails are correct but flat, and steal the shape.
>
> **Caveat:** the styling notes are inferred from where the writing clearly punches, not read off
> the original formatting. Treat them as a reading of the rhythm rather than a spec.

---

## 1. Pacing (the single biggest thing)

**One sentence per paragraph. Often one phrase.**

Almost every paragraph in Shelby's emails is one sentence. Sometimes one fragment. Blank line after every line of text. The page is mostly white space.

Example sequence from her "objections" email (May 6, 2025):

> Like, 7-figures paid.
>
> (Just ask my accountant. Or the 2,500+ women I've trained who are killing it in sales right now.)
>
> So lemme put you on real quick...
>
> These 3 little phrases?

Five "paragraphs" in what would otherwise be three sentences of prose. Each gets its own breathing room.

**Why it works:** the email feels short even when it's 500 words. The reader scans down a column of one-liners, picking up the message without committing to a "block of text."

## 2. Ellipses for mid-thought pause

She uses `...` as the dominant punctuation. Not at the end of sentences — INSIDE sentences, between thoughts.

> Hot take incoming, [first name]...
>
> I kinda hate Christmas movies.
>
> And before you crucify me...
>
> Let me explain.
>
> Because it's not the movies themselves... it's the PLOT.

The ellipsis creates a beat. You read it, pause, then read the punch. It's a vocal-cadence translator.

**This is the single most distinctive Shelby format mark.** More than the emojis, more than the caps. Heavy ellipsis usage.

## 3. Strategic ALL CAPS

Single words or short phrases in ALL CAPS for emphasis. Usually ONE per paragraph. Almost always a noun, verb, or modifier carrying the emotional weight of the sentence.

From the corpus:
- "what are they worried about **RIGHT NOW**?"
- "Are they buying wine and ice cream at 4pm on a Tuesday? Having a **DAY**."
- "to the point where the other person feels **UNDERSTOOD**."
- "That \"skill\" you've been using for **FREE** your entire life?"
- "it's the **PLOT**"
- "Where you actually had **TIME** for the people and things you care about?"
- "**ONE** skill that gives you options."
- "**HOLY SH\*T**, you just made the **BEST** decision of your life!"
- "the **EXACT** roadmap to the life you keep saying you want"
- "**NO REPLAY** for this masterclass. **ZERO. NADA**..."
- "**NEVER** make yourself financially dependent on a man."

Pattern: one CAPS word per beat. The CAPS word IS the emotional click. Use sparingly so it keeps weight.

## 4. Strategic bold / italic (inferred)

- **Punch lines on their own line** (almost certainly bolded): "Girl.", "Yeah.", "Babe.", "Like???"
- **Direct asks** (likely bolded): "I need you to actually save your spot, and show TF up!"
- **The contrast pivot** (likely bolded or italicized): "But here's the tea nobody's talking about..."
- **Italicized asides** (likely italics): "(Just ask my accountant.)", "(No shade to small-town life. Or sourdough guys)"
- **Numbered list action items** (likely bold on the verbs): "**1.** Block off your calendar", "**2.** Set like 5 alarms"


## 5. Emojis — moderate, scattered, decorative

OCR mangles emojis (renders as "75", "17", "ल", "ते"), but they ARE present throughout. From context I can tell she uses:
- Sparkles / fire / heart emojis at the end of certain emphatic lines
- The occasional emoji as a substitute for a word ("Bread Boy" gets an emoji)
- Often paired with a key phrase, not at the end of every paragraph
- 1-3 emojis per email is typical

She does NOT spray emojis everywhere. They're placed at moments of emphasis, like a verbal flourish.

## 6. Opener formula

Cold-open with a hook, set up a story or POV, then earn the click.

Patterns from the corpus:
- **Conditional invitation:** "Okay, this is gonna sound weird, but stay with me..."
- **Hot take:** "Hot take incoming, the operator..."
- **Calling them out:** "the operator, I've invited you to my free, live training like... 47 times at this point"
- **Current event hook:** "OK but can we talk about the CEO who just became the most famous cheater on the internet?"
- **Direct congrats:** "Hey the operator! HOLY SH\*T, you just made the BEST decision of your life!"
- **Hey the operator...** with ellipsis pause

The opener ALWAYS uses the first name. Followed by either an ellipsis or an exclamation.

## 7. Self-correction mid-flow

She breaks her own sentences. This is a voice thing more than a format thing, but it's structural:

> "It's the single most valuable skill in sales.
> 
> Actually, scratch that...
> 
> It's the single most valuable skill in LIFE."

The "scratch that" → second pass pattern. Creates the illusion of thinking in real time.

## 8. Rhetorical question stacking

Stacks 2-5 short rhetorical questions in a row, then answers them.

> Are they checking their phone anxiously? Probably running late for something.
>
> Are they buying wine and ice cream at 4pm on a Tuesday? Having a DAY.
>
> Are they snapping at their kid while simultaneously apologizing to the cashier? Overwhelmed and trying to hold it together.

Or:
> A life where work didn't drain you...
>
> Where money didn't stress you...
>
> Where you actually had TIME for the people and things you care about?

The stack builds emotional momentum. Each question is one paragraph.

## 9. Numbered action lists

When she wants the reader to actually DO something, she numbers it. Usually 3 items max.

> 1. Block off your calendar for 05/15/2025 at 8pm EST
> 2. Set like 5 alarms on your phone (I'm not kidding)
> 3. Tell your boo/bestie/dog that you're unavailable bc you're about to become a BOSS

Parentheticals soften the imperative ("I'm not kidding").

## 10. Sign-off formula

Always the same. Two lines. Sometimes adds a flourish before.

> Love ya,
> Shelby xo

Or:
> Love ya, Shelby xo

The "xo" is consistent. Lowercase intentional.

## 11. P.S. is mandatory and usually the strongest line

Every email has a P.S. The P.S. either:
- **Reframes the message** as identity / superpower: "P.S. That thing you do where you 'just know' what someone needs? That's not a quirk... It's a superpower..."
- **Adds urgency / scarcity:** "P.S. Listen up, pookie! Last time we did this, we had to turn people away because we hit capacity WAY faster than expected."
- **Adds a callback** that ties back to the opener

Ogilvy was right: the P.S. is the second-most-read line after the subject. Shelby uses it as a second-chance hook.

## What transfers, and what has to be adapted

### STEAL (universal format mechanics)

- One-sentence paragraphs.
- Heavy ellipsis use (`...`) for mid-thought pause and beats.
- Strategic ALL CAPS on ONE key word per paragraph.
- Bold on punch lines, direct asks, and action verbs in numbered lists.
- Italics on parentheticals and soft asides.
- Numbered lists for action sequences, three items maximum.
- Conversational opener using the first name plus an ellipsis or exclamation.
- Self-correction mid-flow ("Actually, scratch that...") used sparingly.
- Rhetorical question stacks, two to five short questions each on its own line, for momentum.
- The P.S. is mandatory. Use it as a reframe, an urgency beat, or a callback.
- Sign-off pattern: "[warm phrase], [Name]".

### ADAPT (everything that carries a register)

Each of these lands differently for every audience, so decide them per client from the brand
voice document rather than copying what worked here.

- **Tone of address.** Her "girl / babe / boo" is one specific relationship with one specific
  audience. Write down what the client actually calls their reader, and use only that.
- **Slang.** None of hers transfers. A borrowed idiom is the fastest way to sound like someone
  else wrote the email.
- **Emoji density.** She runs one to three per email. Set a number per client and hold it.
- **Sign-off.** Warmth level is a voice decision, not a format one.
- **P.S. content.** The structural rule is that it exists and does work. What kind of work is
  the client's call.

### DO NOT TRANSFER

Profanity, sexual framing, and anything that reads as a persona rather than a person. These are
load-bearing for her and wrong almost everywhere else. If a line only works because of who is
saying it, it is voice, not format, and this document is about format.

---

## How to use this

Take a client email that is correct but flat, and apply the mechanics in the STEAL list without
touching a single word of their vocabulary. The test is that it reads faster and lands harder
while still sounding like the same person. If it starts sounding like Shelby, you have copied
voice by mistake and it needs reverting.
